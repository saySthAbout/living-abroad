Before applying for an Australian visa, including the Skilled Independent visa (subclass 189), an applicant must consider whether they meet the character requirements set out under section 501 of the Migration Act 1958. The Department of Home Affairs may deny entry to Australia if an applicant has criminal convictions or charges (in Australia or overseas), and may cancel a visa at any time if the holder no longer meets the character requirements.

When applying, an applicant must declare all criminal conduct they have been charged with (and is still awaiting legal action) or convicted of, in any country; answer all questions truthfully; and provide all requested information. After applying, the department may ask for a police certificate, a completed Form 80 (Personal particulars for character assessment), a completed Form 1563 (Statement of Character), a military certificate and/or Form 1399 (Declaration of Service), or a letter of good conduct from an employer.

An applicant may fail the "character test" (as defined in section 501(6) of the Migration Act) if they: have a substantial criminal record; have been convicted of certain crimes involving people under 18; have escaped immigration detention or committed an offence connected to detention or escape; are or have been associated with a person, group, or organisation reasonably suspected of criminal conduct; are reasonably suspected of involvement in serious international offences (such as people smuggling, trafficking, genocide, war crimes, torture, or slavery) whether or not convicted; show through past or present conduct that they are not of good character; are reasonably suspected of posing a risk of criminal conduct, harassment, or inciting community discord while in Australia; are subject to an adverse ASIO security assessment; or are subject to an Interpol notice suggesting a risk to the Australian community.

If serious criminal offences have been committed, it is unlikely a visa will be granted — officers weigh this against Ministerial Direction No.110, which sets out government and community expectations for how criminal history should factor into visa decisions.
